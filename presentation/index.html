A systematic review brings together the evidence for a particular question. We start with a protocol setting out how we'll do that. We search for studies, screen them for relevance, assess their quality and extract the information we need. Then we synthesise the findings and assess how certain we are about the evidence overall. Finally, we write up the review. Those are the stages we'll use to organise the tools.

To think about how AI tools fit into those stages, I'm going to draw on the RAISE guidelines. RAISE stands for Responsible use of AI in evidence synthesis. The guidance was developed by researchers working in evidence synthesis, including people from Cochrane and Campbell. It brings together research evidence and what we know about how these technologies work. That gives us a useful basis for thinking about where they can help, and where we need to be more careful. The guidance is continuously evolving as the tools and evidence develop.

Slide 4 · Approved script

RAISE uses four broad categories for AI tools.

First are rule-based algorithms. These follow rules written by people. For example, a tool might compare titles, authors and other details to identify duplicate references. The rules are often quite transparent, so we can see what led to a particular result. But we still need to know whether they work for the records we give the tool.

Next is unsupervised machine learning. These methods look for patterns without being given labelled examples. They might group abstracts into topics, helping us get a sense of a body of literature. The patterns depend on the data and the choices made in setting up the method.

5 / 21

Slide 5 · Approved script

Supervised machine learning does learn from labelled examples. Some models come already trained; others learn from decisions we make during the review. For example, our decisions about which records are relevant during screening can train a model to move other likely relevant records towards the top of the screening queue. How well it does that depends on the data and the task we ask it to perform. A model that works well on one set of studies may not work as well on another.

Finally, there are generative large language models, or LLMs. These are also machine-learning models, trained on large amounts of data. They predict what is likely to come next in a piece of text. That lets them generate both language and code in response to our instructions. For example, we might ask one to help write R code for an analysis. The output can vary, and something that reads well can still be wrong. So we need to look at how it performs on the particular review task.

Slide 6 · Approved script

An evaluation also needs to be independent of training and development. Suppose we evaluate an LLM using a published review, but that review was already in its training data. Good results could overstate how well it will perform on a new review. When the training data aren't fully disclosed, as is often the case with LLMs, it can be hard to establish whether that overlap exists. This doesn't mean the outputs are necessarily wrong; it means we have less confidence in what the evaluation tells us about performance on new material.

Slide 7 · Approved script

RAISE uses five recommendation categories. These apply to a particular use of a tool, rather than approving the whole product.

“Acceptable for use” means we can use the outputs directly, while accounting for the tool's limitations and potential biases.

“Human verification required” means a person needs to check the outputs carefully and correct them where necessary.

“Requires validation within the review” means we need to test performance in our review. We use a suitable benchmark for comparison, and set a threshold for acceptable performance in advance.

“Exploratory and supplementary use” means using it to develop ideas that we then substantially refine, or adding it alongside established methods without replacing them.

And “Not acceptable for use” means we should not rely on those outputs for that task.

8 / 21

Slide 8 · Approved script

Verification means checking the actual output. If an LLM writes R code for an analysis, someone who understands the code and the methods needs to check it and the results. Does it carry out the analysis we intended? Simply running without an error isn't enough.

Validation means evaluating how well a tool or workflow performs on its intended task. Existing evaluations may provide sufficient evidence if they are robust and apply to our intended use. Where RAISE requires validation within the review, we need to test performance in our own review context.

For screening with early stopping, we need to evaluate the screening and stopping procedure against an appropriate human-screened reference. We also need to decide beforehand what performance is acceptable. An important measure is recall: the proportion of eligible records actually found.

This assessment does not necessarily mean screening every record. A statistically justified sampling or stopping method may be suitable, but the assessment can still use up much of the time we hoped to save.

A workflow can need both validation and verification. They serve different purposes: establishing performance and checking the outputs we actually use.

There is another pattern in where the tools appear. The rule-based examples cluster around searching and synthesis. The supervised models are concentrated in screening, quality assessment and extraction, where many tasks involve predicting relevance or identifying information in study reports.

LLMs span a much wider range of stages. ChatGPT, for example, appears in seven of the eight stages in this map. Its ability to work with language and code gives it many possible roles, although the recommendation depends on the particular task.

So understanding the type of AI helps us make sense of the pattern of uses and of recommendations. We also need to look at what we're asking it to do, and what checking or validation that requires in practice.

Slide 14 · Approved script

If we use a model to put likely relevant records near the top of the queue, but still screen every record, it's changing the order of our work. Even if it ranks something badly, we will still get to it. That use is acceptable.

But if we stop partway through because the remaining records look unlikely to be relevant, we are now relying on its predictions when we leave some records unscreened. If its predictions are wrong, we could miss eligible studies. So we need to validate the screening process, including the decision about when to stop.

The model could be doing exactly the same ranking in both cases. What changes is how much we rely on that ranking, and what happens if it's wrong.

Slide 16 · Approved script

RAISE currently considers it unacceptable to use an LLM to synthesise findings across studies for a formal review.

For example, if we're reviewing whether a treatment works, we need to decide which studies can reasonably be combined and how to analyse their results. We need to account for uncertainty and differences between studies, and check how sensitive our conclusions are to the analysis. A convincing-sounding textual summary from an LLM doesn't establish that this work has been done reliably.

That's different from asking an LLM to help write R code for an analysis we've chosen. We can inspect the code and check whether it carries out the intended analysis and produces appropriate results. RAISE allows this with knowledgeable human verification.

In qualitative reviews, RAISE allows assistance with coding or identifying themes, subject to validation and verification. That is different from delegating the synthesis across studies, although RAISE doesn't fully define the boundary between those roles.

Slide 17 · Approved script

RAISE also considers complete reviews carried out by agentic systems unacceptable. These systems work through multiple tasks, and errors can carry forward from one stage to the next. Human feedback between stages doesn't remove this restriction. The restriction applies to complete review workflows. Using an agent for an individual task needs to be assessed separately.

For uses that are potentially acceptable, the next question is whether a particular tool is suitable for our review.

Slide 18 · Approved script

RAISE sets out five questions for assessing a tool. Let's apply them to the screening example. Suppose our team wants to use prioritised screening and stop before every record has been screened.

What is the purpose of the tool? We're asking it to do more than reorder records. We want to use a stopping procedure to decide when we've screened enough. That is the workflow we need to assess.

Where have the training, testing and validation data come from? In this screening workflow, the model learns from our own inclusion and exclusion decisions and updates as we screen. We need to consider the quality of those decisions and how the training examples were selected. Separately, we need to know how the screening and stopping methods were evaluated, and whether that evidence applies to our review.

Slide 20 · Approved script

Does the team have the expertise and resource to use the AI tool effectively, and is the usability acceptable? Can we operate the tool, assess its performance and investigate mistakes? We need the skills and time to do that, and a workflow the team can use reliably. Any expected time saving needs to account for those tasks.

Is there sufficient transparency around licenses and other available documentation? Can we find clear instructions, understand the conditions of access and establish what happens to the records we upload? We also need enough information to report how we used the tool, including its version and settings.

If the evidence for stopping is missing, that remains a gap to resolve before we rely on it.
