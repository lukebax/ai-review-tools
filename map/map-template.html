About RAISE

RAISE means Responsible use of AI in evidence SynthEsis. Its three papers help reviewers use AI responsibly, developers build and evaluate tools, and review teams select tools for particular tasks.

These expert-based recommendations draw on expertise from Cochrane, Campbell, UCL’s EPPI Centre and others, with open consultation. Cochrane advises its authors to follow RAISE.

RAISE is living guidance: the current drafts remain open to consultation and revision. Updates depend on new evidence and the authors’ capacity, with no fixed schedule. The RAISE collection provides the documents and version history.

What do the review stages mean?

The eight rows follow a review from planning its methods to reporting its findings. They separate tasks such as finding studies, deciding which to include and judging confidence in the results. Not every review type uses every stage. Teams can revisit stages; a row’s presence does not imply permission to automate it.

Writing a protocol
Plan the review: refine the question, explore the topic, estimate resources and specify methods before starting.
The Search
Find studies: scope the literature, design and adapt database searches, retrieve records, follow citations, check that known studies are found and remove duplicates.
Screening for relevance
Check titles, abstracts and full reports against eligibility criteria. Prioritising records and deciding to stop screening early are separate choices.
Quality assessment
Assess study reporting and risk of bias. Complete reporting does not necessarily mean low risk of bias.
Data extraction
Collect study details and results, including information in text, tables and figures.
Data synthesis
Bring findings together through quantitative or qualitative analysis. Tools may also help with evidence maps or analysis code. Text summaries alone do not establish a sound synthesis.
Certainty of evidence assessment
Judge confidence in the combined evidence or synthesised finding, using an appropriate framework. This goes beyond assessing individual studies; reviewers remain responsible.
Writing a manuscript
Draft and revise the report, including plain-language summaries of human-written findings. Writing assistance does not replace analysis or interpretation.

This sequence adapts RAISE 3 v4, Table 2 (pp.12–19): the first seven rows follow its review-task sections, while Writing a manuscript brings together drafting, revisions and plain-language summaries from Text Generation. Translation is shown where it supports human work in four stages; those cells explain the interpretation.

What do the AI categories mean?

The map retains RAISE’s four technology families. Unsupervised and supervised learning share a visual Machine Learning heading, with separate subheadings. The categories reflect RAISE’s classification of the listed functions, rather than every feature of a product:

Rule-based algorithms
Follow explicit rules, such as matching references or converting search queries. Symbolic AI represents knowledge and rules explicitly. RAISE’s broad group also includes conventional statistics and visualisation; not every statistical calculation is AI.
Unsupervised machine learning
Finds patterns without predefined labels, for example clusters of papers or topics. Clustering is one use, not the full definition. The narrow subheading abbreviates “Unsupervised” to Unsup.
Supervised machine learning
Learns from labelled examples to classify, rank or locate information. Some models come already trained; others learn from decisions made by the review team. Classification is one use, not the full definition; a tool can also combine learned components with fixed rules.
Generative large language models (LLMs)
Neural-network models trained on extensive data to generate text or code; they can also support screening and extraction. They can hallucinate, generating plausible but false or unsupported content. Chatbots provide conversation; agentic systems can plan and carry out multiple steps. RAISE treats these as modes of LLM use.

These are practical display groups: LLMs are themselves machine-learning models, and tools can combine approaches. Cell details retain the more specific Table 2 labels, including modes of use, and explain implementation qualifications. Google Translate and DeepL are display exceptions: their source label is Generative and other machine learning approaches, so their placement does not imply exclusively LLM-based implementations.

Sources: RAISE 2 v4, pp.6–7; RAISE 3 v4, p.3 and Table 2. Background on symbolic, probabilistic and neural approaches: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, §§3.2–3.4 and 4.1.

What do the recommendations mean?

The colours describe the conditions RAISE places on a particular use. Verification means checking outputs; validation means testing whether the intended procedure performs well enough. These recommendations rate tasks and workflows, not whole products. Reviewers remain responsible in every category.

The map presents selected tools and tasks from RAISE’s guidance. The patterns reflect their functions, methods, maturity and supporting evidence. These factors vary together, so the colours do not independently establish that one AI family is safer than another.

One stage can contain several functions or workflows with different recommendations. A split cell shows two of these recommendations; its diagonal halves do not represent proportions, confidence or importance. Definitions adapted from RAISE 3 v4, Table 1 (p.11).

Why can recommendations differ within a review stage?

A stage contains several tasks, and those tasks can need different safeguards. In Data synthesis, RAISE rates chatbot code assistance as requiring human verification: a knowledgeable reviewer can inspect the method and outputs. Qualitative-analysis assistance from MAXQDA and NVivo requires validation within the review. Asking an LLM to derive findings across studies is a different task, rated Not acceptable for use. Google Translate and DeepL support reviewers by translating material; they are not performing the synthesis.

Existing evidence must apply to the intended task and way of working. Stable tasks make it easier to build evidence that can be reused across reviews. Evaluations from other reviews or other fields can be relevant when the task, material, system and workflow are sufficiently similar. Review-specific criteria can limit that transfer, but do not make external evidence irrelevant. Assess the whole intended procedure, including any independent human review. Adding a human does not by itself establish adequate performance or remove RAISE’s requirement for validation within the review.

A split cell therefore represents different functions or workflows within the same tool and stage. Select it to see which recommendation applies to each use, rather than treating either colour as a rating of the whole product.

One boundary remains uncertain: RAISE does not fully resolve where qualitative coding or theme assistance becomes substantive synthesis. Read MAXQDA and NVivo’s cell qualifications alongside the wider restrictions in RAISE 3 v4, Table 2 (p.17).

Why are some uses not acceptable?

RAISE considers current LLM-generated cross-study syntheses unreliable because a convincing summary can leave out the methodological work needed to justify the conclusions. Imagine several studies investigating a treatment. A review must decide whether their results can meaningfully be combined, account for differences between them, and examine how uncertain the overall finding is. Simply blending the papers’ summaries can miss these steps and give a misleading impression of what the evidence shows. This matters when combining findings in words as well as in a statistical meta-analysis.

RAISE classifies relying on LLM-generated synthesis across studies as Not acceptable for use in a formal review. Google AI Mode, Elicit, Perplexity and Asta are its examples of this use. The recommendation concerns this specified use; it does not prohibit every function of those products.

RAISE treats statistical synthesis with meta and metafor differently, although they also combine results across studies. They perform specified statistical calculations on data supplied by reviewers, using analytical choices that can be documented, inspected and changed. This provides a traceable way of combining evidence, rather than inferring an overall answer from the wording of papers. RAISE rates this Acceptable for use; reviewers still have to choose appropriate data and methods and interpret the results correctly.

An LLM can help with that statistical work by drafting analysis code. Here, a knowledgeable reviewer can inspect what the code does and check its outputs against the intended analysis. RAISE permits code assistance with Human verification required: the reviewer checks the actual method, not just whether the final answer sounds plausible. Code that runs can still implement the wrong analysis.

Checking a defined script addresses a specific task; intermediate feedback to an agent does not, by itself, verify a whole review. When an agent links searching and extraction to generating cross-study conclusions, missed studies or incorrect data can feed into the final answer. RAISE also rejects relying on these workflows to produce a complete synthesis, with or without intermediate human input. “Agentic” itself describes planning and acting towards a goal, which can happen within one task. RAISE gives some agentic search support the different rating Exploratory and supplementary use.

Nor does the restriction mean every summary is prohibited: explaining findings from a completed human synthesis is a different task from deriving those findings. RAISE leaves some uncertainty about when qualitative coding or theme assistance becomes substantive synthesis; MAXQDA and NVivo’s cell details retain that qualification.

The evidence supports caution without proving that every product fails every workflow. Shaib et al. found that an older GPT-3 workflow often reached different conclusions from expert-written syntheses; Reason et al., cited by RAISE, showed promise for carefully configured, checked statistical-code workflows. Both have limited settings. RAISE also relies on reasoning about system design; its multi-document summarisation citation is a publishing white paper, not a direct synthesis experiment.

Sources: RAISE 3 v4, pp.3–4, 8–11 and Table 2, pp.13, 17, 19; RAISE 2 v4, pp.6–8. The treatment example illustrates the guidance; the separately identified studies provide additional context.

Is this tool suitable for my review?

A recommendation colour alone cannot establish suitability for your review. Use RAISE’s five assessment questions to consider the specific task, context and proposed way of working.

  1. What is the purpose of the tool? Define what you want the tool to do, whether it will supplement or replace an existing method, and how reviewers will oversee its operations and outputs.
  2. Where have the training, testing and validation data come from? Check whether the data sources are disclosed and sufficiently documented, and whether the data match your review’s topics, documents and languages. If the model learns from your review’s decisions, check their quality and how the training examples were selected. For LLMs, check that the prompt-development process is recorded and accessible. Look for gaps or biases, and separation between development and performance testing.
  3. Is the tool validated and performing sufficiently well for use? Assess the quality and relevance of existing evaluations, including the tested version, comparator (what the tool was compared with) and task-appropriate measures. Decide in advance what performance is adequate and whether further validation is needed. Monitor performance and reassess when the model, prompts or context change.
  4. Does the team have the expertise and resource to use the AI tool effectively, and is the usability acceptable? Consider skills, training, time, cost, technical support and compatibility with your workflow. Where human checking is required, ensure the team has the expertise, source access and time to detect errors and omissions, and correct outputs reliably.
  5. Is there sufficient transparency around licenses and other available documentation? Check terms of use, privacy, rights over uploaded material and outputs, funding and conflicts of interest. Check whether uploaded material is retained or used for model training, and whether you can opt out or delete it. Consider availability, maintenance and relevant legal, ethical, environmental and social implications.

Keep the assessment proportionate to the proposed use and the consequences of errors. Record the reasons for deciding to proceed, proceed with safeguards and monitoring, or defer use until further evidence or information is available. The review team remains responsible for the decision.

What does the map leave out?

The grid organises named tools by individual review stages. Some RAISE 3 Table 2 entries do not fit that structure: they describe unnamed methods, whole-review workflows or interaction with existing evidence. Their guidance is explained here instead.

Two methods without a named tool

RAISE describes checking preclinical reports for prescribed text patterns, called regular expressions or regex. These rules flag reporting details for human assessment rather than settle the judgement. It also describes extracting study details with rules that find candidate information and a language model that filters it, linking a research example extracting intervention details.

Both methods require validation within the review (RAISE 3 v4, Table 2, pp.15–16). Neither has a column because Table 2 names no separate tool for these entries. STEED and Pre-ROB are separately named examples and retain their own columns.

End-to-end evidence-synthesis workflows

RAISE lists Elicit and Perplexity for workflows producing a complete evidence synthesis, with or without intermediate human input, and rates this Not acceptable for use (RAISE 3 v4, Table 2, p.19). The tools appear in the grid for individual tasks, but this whole-review use spans stages. The synthesis explanation sets out why it receives this recommendation.

Using the map with a keyboard

Use Tab to move through the page’s controls. Once you reach a map cell, use the arrow keys to move between cells. Home and End reach the first and last visible tool in the current row.

Open the AI category or Review stage filter with Enter or Space, then use Tab to reach its checkboxes and Space to select any combination. Escape closes the filter. No selection shows all categories or stages.

Press Enter or Space to open the cell’s details. Use Tab to reach links and the close button; Shift + Tab moves backwards. Escape closes the details and returns focus to the cell. On narrow screens, focus stays inside the details panel until you close it.
