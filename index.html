About RAISE

RAISE means Responsible use of AI in evidence SynthEsis. Its three papers help reviewers use AI responsibly, developers build and evaluate tools, and review teams select tools for particular tasks.

These recommendations draw on expertise from Cochrane, Campbell, UCL’s EPPI Centre and others, with open consultation. Cochrane advises its authors to follow RAISE.

RAISE is living guidance: the current drafts remain open to consultation and revision. Updates depend on new evidence and the authors’ capacity, with no fixed schedule. The RAISE collection provides the documents and version history.

About this map and its sources

This independent adaptation by Luke Baxter organises RAISE 3 Table 2 into named tools, features and resources across eight review stages. It is not an official RAISE publication or a live product catalogue.

Read each recommendation alongside its task. RAISE often rates a task and technology type, rather than evaluating every product separately. Cell details explain functions, interpretations and implementation qualifications. Tool links describe features; they do not establish validation, endorsement or current availability. Links were checked in October 2026.

Organising the guidance by stage requires some adaptation: Writing a manuscript groups text-generation tasks, and Google Translate and DeepL appear in five stages where translation could support reviewers. Those placements are interpretations, not separate validations; the stage and AI-category explanations give the details.

Because columns represent named tools, features and resources, two methods without named tools have no separate columns. “What does the map leave out?” explains these and the uses spanning or falling outside individual review stages.

What do the review stages mean?

The eight rows follow a review from planning its methods to reporting its findings. They separate tasks such as finding studies, deciding which to include and judging confidence in the results. Teams can revisit stages; a row’s presence does not imply permission to automate it.

Writing a protocol
Plan the review: refine the question, explore the topic, estimate resources and specify methods before starting.
The Search
Find studies: scope the literature, design and adapt database searches, retrieve records, follow citations, check that known studies are found and remove duplicates.
Screening for relevance
Check titles, abstracts and full reports against eligibility criteria. Prioritising records and deciding to stop screening early are separate choices.
Quality assessment
Assess study reporting and risk of bias. Complete reporting does not necessarily mean low risk of bias.
Data extraction
Collect study details and results, including information in text, tables and figures.
Data synthesis
Bring findings together through quantitative or qualitative analysis. Tools may also help with evidence maps or analysis code. Text summaries alone do not establish a sound synthesis.
Certainty of evidence assessment
Judge confidence in the combined evidence or synthesised finding, using an appropriate framework. This goes beyond assessing individual studies; reviewers remain responsible.
Writing a manuscript
Draft and revise the report, including plain-language summaries of human-written findings. Writing assistance does not replace analysis or interpretation.

This sequence adapts RAISE 3 v4, Table 2 (pp.12–19): the first seven rows follow its review-task sections, while Writing a manuscript brings together drafting, revisions and plain-language summaries from Text Generation. Translation is shown where it supports human work in five stages; those cells explain the interpretation.

What do the AI categories mean?

The map retains RAISE’s four technology families. Unsupervised and supervised learning share a visual Machine Learning heading, with separate subheadings. The categories reflect RAISE’s classification of the listed functions, rather than every feature of a product:

Rule-based algorithms (symbolic AI)
Follow explicit rules, such as matching references or converting search queries. Symbolic AI represents knowledge and rules explicitly. RAISE’s broad group also includes conventional statistics and visualisation; not every statistical calculation is AI.
Unsupervised machine learning
Finds patterns without predefined labels, for example clusters of papers or topics. Clustering is one use, not the full definition. The narrow subheading abbreviates “Unsupervised” to Unsup.
Supervised machine learning
Learns from labelled examples to classify, rank or locate information. Classification is one use, not the full definition; a tool can also combine learned components with fixed rules.
Generative large language models (LLMs)
Neural-network models trained on extensive data to generate text or code; they can also support screening and extraction. Chatbots provide conversation; agentic systems can plan and carry out multiple steps. RAISE treats these as modes of LLM use.

These are practical display groups: LLMs are themselves machine-learning models, and tools can combine approaches. Cell details retain RAISE’s classification for each function and explain implementation qualifications. Google Translate and DeepL are display exceptions: their source label is Generative and other machine learning approaches, so their placement does not imply exclusively LLM-based implementations.

Sources: RAISE 2 v4, pp.6–7; RAISE 3 v4, p.3 and Table 2. Background on symbolic, probabilistic and neural approaches: Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, §§3.2–3.4 and 4.1.

What do the recommendations mean?

The colours describe the conditions RAISE places on a particular use. Verification means checking outputs; validation means testing whether the intended procedure performs well enough. These recommendations rate tasks and workflows, not whole products. Reviewers remain responsible in every category.

One stage can contain several functions or workflows with different recommendations. A split cell shows two of these recommendations; its diagonal halves do not represent proportions, confidence or importance. Definitions adapted from RAISE 3 v4, Table 1 (p.11).

Why can recommendations differ within a review stage?

A stage contains several tasks, and those tasks can need different safeguards. In Data synthesis, drafting analysis code allows a knowledgeable reviewer to inspect the method and outputs, so chatbot code assistance requires human verification. Qualitative-analysis assistance from MAXQDA and NVivo requires validation within the review. Asking an LLM to derive findings across studies is a different task, rated Not acceptable for use. Google Translate and DeepL support reviewers by translating material; they are not performing the synthesis.

A split cell therefore represents different functions or workflows within the same tool and stage. Select it to see which recommendation applies to each use, rather than treating either colour as a rating of the whole product.

One boundary remains uncertain: RAISE does not fully resolve where qualitative coding or theme assistance becomes substantive synthesis. Read MAXQDA and NVivo’s cell qualifications alongside the wider restrictions in RAISE 3 v4, Table 2 (p.17).

Why are some uses not acceptable?

RAISE considers current LLM-generated cross-study syntheses unreliable because a convincing summary can leave out the methodological work needed to justify the conclusions. Imagine several studies investigating a treatment. A review must decide whether their results can meaningfully be combined, account for differences between them, and examine how uncertain the overall finding is. Simply blending the papers’ summaries can miss these steps and give a misleading impression of what the evidence shows. This matters when combining findings in words as well as in a statistical meta-analysis.

RAISE therefore classifies relying on LLM-generated synthesis across studies as Not acceptable for use in a formal review. Google AI Mode, Elicit, Perplexity and Asta are its examples of this use. The recommendation concerns the work delegated to the tool; it does not prohibit every function of those products.

That explains why meta and metafor receive a different recommendation, even though they also combine results across studies. They perform specified statistical calculations on data supplied by reviewers, using analytical choices that can be documented, inspected and changed. This provides a traceable way of combining evidence, rather than inferring an overall answer from the wording of papers. RAISE rates this Acceptable for use; reviewers still have to choose appropriate data and methods and interpret the results correctly.

An LLM can help with that statistical work by drafting analysis code. Here, a knowledgeable reviewer can inspect what the code does and check its outputs against the intended analysis. This is why code assistance receives Human verification required: the reviewer checks the actual method, not just whether the final answer sounds plausible. Code that runs can still implement the wrong analysis.

Checking a defined script addresses a specific task; intermediate feedback to an agent does not, by itself, verify a whole review. When an agent links searching and extraction to generating cross-study conclusions, missed studies or incorrect data can feed into the final answer. RAISE therefore also rejects relying on these workflows to produce a complete synthesis, with or without intermediate human input. “Agentic” itself describes planning and acting towards a goal, which can happen within one task. RAISE gives some agentic search support the different rating Exploratory and supplementary use.

Nor does the restriction mean every summary is prohibited: explaining findings from a completed human synthesis is a different task from deriving those findings. RAISE leaves some uncertainty about when qualitative coding or theme assistance becomes substantive synthesis; MAXQDA and NVivo’s cell details retain that qualification.

The evidence supports caution without proving that every product fails every workflow. Shaib et al. found that an older GPT-3 workflow often reached different conclusions from expert-written syntheses; Reason et al., cited by RAISE, showed promise for carefully configured, checked statistical-code workflows. Both have limited settings. RAISE also relies on reasoning about system design; its multi-document summarisation citation is a publishing white paper, not a direct synthesis experiment.

Sources: RAISE 3 v4, pp.3–4, 8–11 and Table 2, pp.13, 17, 19; RAISE 2 v4, pp.6–8. The treatment example illustrates the guidance; the separately identified studies provide additional context.

Before using a tool

A colour alone cannot establish suitability. Plan how the team will evaluate the specific use, detect errors and remain accountable.

  1. Plan the use. Before supplying material, check confidentiality, data handling, permissions and copyright. Where validation is required, set a task-specific threshold and benchmark in advance, and evaluate the actual human–AI workflow, including checking and correction effort. Ensure sufficient skills, time, resources and a fallback.
  2. Keep performance tests independent. When testing performance, use unseen material matching the review’s population, documents and decisions. Keep tuning examples separate; further tuning requires testing on fresh independent material. For generative models, assess consistency across runs and consequential errors, including unsupported outputs and missing information. Where robust within-review validation is required but cannot be assured, limit use to exploratory or supplementary purposes.
  3. Make verification workable. Where verification is required, a qualified person needs time and source access to correct errors; fluent wording is insufficient. Checking can miss or introduce errors, and AI suggestions can influence judgement, so verification does not guarantee accuracy.
  4. Document and reassess. Record relevant versions, settings, prompts, inputs, outputs, dates and human interventions. Disclose substantial AI use, evaluation, limitations and effects on methods. Monitor performance during use and reassess after changes to the tool, prompts, task or workflow.
  5. Meet wider responsibilities. Weigh environmental and social costs, accessibility and likely benefit. Standards for searching, selection, appraisal, analysis and reporting still apply; the review team remains accountable.

What does the map leave out?

The grid organises named tools by individual review stages. Some RAISE 3 Table 2 entries do not fit that structure: they describe unnamed methods, whole-review workflows or interaction with existing evidence. Their guidance is explained here instead.

Two methods without a named tool

RAISE describes checking preclinical reports for prescribed text patterns, called regular expressions or regex. These rules flag reporting details for human assessment rather than settle the judgement. It also describes extracting study details with rules that find candidate information and a language model that filters it, linking a research example extracting intervention details.

Both methods require validation within the review (RAISE 3 v4, Table 2, pp.15–16). Neither has a column because Table 2 names no separate tool for these entries. STEED and Pre-ROB are separately named examples and retain their own columns.

End-to-end evidence-synthesis workflows

RAISE lists Elicit and Perplexity for workflows producing a complete evidence synthesis, with or without intermediate human input, and rates this Not acceptable for use (RAISE 3 v4, Table 2, p.19). The tools appear in the grid for individual tasks, but this whole-review use spans stages. The synthesis explanation sets out why it receives this recommendation.
